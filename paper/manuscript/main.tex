\title{What Helps When Telemetry Lies?\\Four Pre-Registered Tests of Evidence Acquisition\\under Source Compromise}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Aakash G.S.}
\IEEEauthorblockA{gsaakash@outlook.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A threat hunter with a fixed budget must choose which telemetry sources to read, knowing that some of them may be
controlled by the attacker. Many acquisition rules have been proposed for this setting, but they are rarely compared
at equal spend, against attackers they were not designed for, with the comparison fixed before the data exist. We run
four pre-registered experiments that each change one ingredient of a Bayesian acquisition policy while holding the
shared posterior, the terminal decision, the horizon and the spend fixed. Three run on procedurally generated worlds
(\numnEL, \numnET\ and \numnEU\ policy-episodes) and one on a public industrial-control dataset. Two ingredients fail their
gates: valuing integrity probes by their decision consequences adds nothing over hypothesis information under the same
model (relative regret reduction $\numLC$, 95\% CI [$\numLClo$, $\numLChi$]), and randomised or minimax acquisition does
not beat a non-robust policy on a held-out attacker ($\numTR$ and $\numTM$). One ingredient passes every gate: scoring
evidence by hypothesis information under a joint posterior over the hypothesis and every source's compromise state
cuts regret on a held-out attacker by $\numUR$ [$\numURlo$, $\numURhi$] relative to reliability-weighted information
gain and by $\numUJ$ [$\numUJlo$, $\numUJhi$] relative to joint-state information gain. The gain is large against
cover-up and decoy attackers, small against misattribution, and absent against an attacker that adapts to the
defender's queries, where random acquisition is best. A replication on the HAI dataset was invalid by its own
pre-registered check: the mapped evidence did not beat a decision made from the prior alone. The joint-belief result
is therefore simulator-only.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
threat hunting, adversarial telemetry, active hypothesis testing, value of information, pre-registration, negative results

\section{Introduction}

Attackers tamper with the evidence defenders rely on. Log deletion, forwarder sabotage and sensor spoofing are
catalogued techniques (MITRE ATT\&CK T1562, ``Impair Defenses'' \cite{attackt1562}), and stale-value spoofing of
process variables is a standard attack on industrial control systems \cite{shin2020hai}. A hunter who investigates an
alert under a budget therefore faces two coupled questions: which source to read next, and how far to believe it.

Each piece of this problem has a long history. Choosing the next measurement by expected entropy reduction over a
joint fault model goes back to the General Diagnostic Engine \cite{dekleer1987}; decision-theoretic troubleshooting
values tests by their effect on the repair decision \cite{heckerman1995}; active sequential hypothesis testing gives
asymptotically optimal sensing rules \cite{naghshvar2013}; and Byzantine detection studies inference when some sensors
lie \cite{marano2009,soltanmohammadi2012}. Recent work combines a belief over compromised sensors with active probing in
cyber-physical control \cite{andersson2026} and learns cost-aware sensor scheduling with a Byzantine detector
\cite{zhong2025}.

What is missing is a controlled answer to a practical question: \emph{which ingredient of such a policy actually
reduces decision errors when sources lie?} A new policy usually differs from its baseline in several ways at once
(belief model, objective, look-ahead, cost handling), is evaluated against the attacker its model assumes, and is
compared at unequal spend. Any of these can manufacture an apparent gain.

We address this with an ablation discipline rather than a new method. Every policy in this paper shares the same
exact posterior, the same Bayes terminal decision and exactly the same spend; policies differ only in how they score
the next action. Each experiment changes one ingredient, names its comparator, fixes a practical threshold
(a 10\% relative cut in regret) and its kill gates in a committed protocol, and is tested on an attacker written
after the policies were frozen.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A spend-matched, pre-registered testbed for evidence acquisition under source compromise: procedurally
generated worlds, six attacker families of which two were held out at design time, and frozen gate code
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item Two pre-registered negative results. Valuing source-integrity probes by their decision consequences adds no
robust gain over hypothesis information under the same joint model, and randomised or minimax acquisition loses to
the non-robust comparator on a held-out attacker (Sec.~\ref{sec:neg}).
\item One scoped positive result. At equal objective, horizon and cost handling, replacing a reliability-weighted
belief with a joint belief over hypothesis and source states reduces regret by $\numUR$ on a held-out attacker; we
report where the gain vanishes (Sec.~\ref{sec:pos}).
\item A real-data attempt on the HAI industrial-control dataset that failed its own validity check, with the reasons,
so that the positive result is reported as simulator-only (Sec.~\ref{sec:hai}).
\end{enumerate}

We do not claim the joint belief model as new; it is classical \cite{dekleer1987,heckerman1995,soltanmohammadi2012}.
The contribution is the controlled evidence about which ingredient matters, and where it stops mattering.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Diagnosis and troubleshooting.} GDE selects measurements by minimum expected entropy over component states
\cite{dekleer1987}; Bayesian-network test selection does the same at scale \cite{zheng2005}; decision-theoretic
troubleshooting values tests by their effect on the repair decision \cite{heckerman1995}. Faults there are not
strategic, and observations of healthy components are trusted. Our joint-state objective $J$ is the GDE criterion and
our decision-value probe $P$ follows the troubleshooting criterion.

\textbf{Sensing with adversarial sources.} Byzantine detection studies fusion when a fraction of sensors lie, mostly
through error exponents and game equilibria with all sensors observed \cite{marano2009}. Soltanmohammadi et al.\ infer
node identity jointly with the hypothesis by EM \cite{soltanmohammadi2012}, the closest inference-side match to our
belief model, but without a choice of which source to read. Andersson and D\'an maintain a Bayesian network over sensor
attack states and choose a probing input that maximises a KL divergence, with the goal of control recovery
\cite{andersson2026}. Zhong et al.\ learn sensor scheduling with probing costs and a separate Byzantine detector
\cite{zhong2025}. MATE estimates per-agent trust as a hidden state for fusion \cite{hallyburton2025}. None of these
ablates the belief model against the objective at equal spend, or evaluates on attackers written after the policy.

\textbf{Active learning with unreliable oracles.} Proactive learning selects among imperfect, cost-varying oracles
\cite{donmez2008}; cost-aware active learning divides information by cost \cite{settles2008,settles2009}. Oracle
noise there is not strategic. Recent work attacks the acquisition step itself \cite{alselect2025,oraclepoison2026};
it does not study which acquisition rule resists such attacks. Robust Bayesian experimental design optimises the worst
case over model misspecification \cite{maximinbed2026}, which motivates our minimax candidate.

\textbf{Security operations.} POMDP formulations plan sensing and defence under noisy alerts
\cite{mccarthy2016,miehling2018} but treat sensor noise as non-strategic. Provenance triage \cite{nodoze2019},
tamper-evident logging \cite{custos2020} and hunting under anti-forensics \cite{hunteragent2026} motivate our integrity
probe and our compromised-source model, but do not choose evidence decision-theoretically.

\section{Problem and Policies}\label{sec:model}

\textbf{State.} A hidden hypothesis $H\in\{\text{benign},\text{intrusion}_1,\text{intrusion}_2\}$ and, for each of
$K$ sources, a state $S_q\in\{\text{healthy},\text{degraded},\text{compromised}\}$. A \emph{query} of source $q$
returns an outcome $o$ indicating a hypothesis; a healthy source reports correctly with probability $\mathrm{acc}_q$, a
degraded one uniformly, and a compromised one (in the defender's model) returns a forged outcome with probability
$0.9$: a cover-up (``benign'') under an intrusion, a false flag under benign. A \emph{probe} of $q$ is an integrity
check (canary, heartbeat or cross-sensor check) that returns PASS or FAIL with state-dependent rates. Compromise is less
likely under benign activity: $P(S_q{=}C\mid\text{benign})=\kappa\,P(S_q{=}C\mid\text{intrusion})$.

\textbf{Decision and regret.} After the budget is spent, the defender takes $D^*=\arg\max_D\mathbb{E}[U(D,H)\mid E]$
over \{dismiss, contain, block\}: 0 if correct, $-L_{\text{miss}}$ for dismissing an intrusion, $-0.5L_{\text{miss}}$ for
the wrong containment, $-1$ for a false alarm. Regret is $U(D^*(H),H)-U(D,H)$ divided by $L_{\text{miss}}$.

\textbf{Shared machinery.} Every policy sees the same exact posterior $P(H,S_1,\dots,S_K\mid E)$, and the simulator,
not the policy, applies the same Bayes decision. Costs are integers with a fixed budget, and every policy must act while
it can afford an action, so every policy spends exactly the budget in every episode (checked in every run). Policies
never see the true hypothesis, source states or attacker identity; leakage tests enforce this.

\textbf{Policies differ only in scoring.} A score has three parts: the \emph{model} used for look-ahead, the
\emph{objective} credited per outcome, and the \emph{horizon}. With horizon 2 and cost-awareness,
$\text{score}(a)=\max\big(V_1(a)/c_a,\ \max_b V_2(a,b)/(c_a+c_b)\big)$. The four policies that matter here are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $R$ (\pol{P3\_RHAT\_COST\_LA2}): reliability-weighted model. Look-ahead treats a source as
$\hat r_q\,p_{\text{nom}}+(1-\hat r_q)\,\text{uniform}$, with $\hat r_q$ the current posterior probability that $q$ is
healthy, and $H$ and $S$ independent. Objective: entropy reduction of $H$.
\item $C$ (\pol{P\_JOINT\_HEIG\_COST}): exact joint model; objective: entropy reduction of $H$.
\item $J$ (\pol{P\_JOINT\_EIG\_COST}): exact joint model; objective: entropy reduction of $(H,S)$, the GDE criterion.
\item $P$ (\pol{P\_PROBE}): exact joint model; objective: expected gain in the Bayes-decision utility (value of
information), falling back to $H$-information on VoI plateaus.
\end{itemize}
All four use horizon 2 and divide by cost. $C$ and $R$ differ only in the belief model; $C$ and $J$ only in the
objective. References include random acquisition, nominal EIG that ignores cost and compromise (\pol{P3\_NOM}), and,
in E2, two robust variants of $J$.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Worlds.} Each unit of analysis is a world drawn from a frozen distribution: $K\sim U\{5..8\}$ sources; source
accuracy $U(0.45,0.95)$; query cost $U\{1..4\}$; probe-cost ratio log-$U(0.25,2)$; compromise prior $U(0.02,0.40)$ with
per-source jitter; benign coupling $\kappa\sim U(0,1)$; probe detection $U(0.5,0.99)$ and false-fail $U(0,0.1)$; loss
ratio $L_{\text{miss}}$ log-$U(1,20)$; budget 12. No world is hand-built.

\textbf{Attackers.} An attacker controls $B\in\{1,2,3\}$ sources and is isolated from the policies. Six families:
\emph{cover-up} (the design family, matching the defender's model); \emph{misattribution} (targets the most trusted
sources and pushes the wrong containment); \emph{trust-harvest} (adaptive: honest until queried, then takes the source
over, lies low after a probe); \emph{decoy-migrate} (held out in E2: a sticky decoy class, migrates when probed);
\emph{collude-cheap} (held out in E3: controls the cheapest sources, which all tell the same lie). Within a world every
policy faces the same truths and random numbers (common random numbers), 12 episodes per (world, condition).

\textbf{Endpoint and statistics.} For candidate $X$ and comparator $Y$, $\bar y_w$ is a world's mean normalised regret
over the primary cells and $\text{RRR}=1-\sum_w\bar y_w(X)/\sum_w\bar y_w(Y)$. A gate passes only if RRR $\ge 0.10$
\emph{and} the one-sided paired-bootstrap lower bound (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed) exceeds 0. The number of worlds
came from a blinded calibration on separate seeds that output only the comparator's mean and the spread of the
difference. E1's calibration asked for 140 worlds; E2 and E3 asked for more than the pre-registered cap of 400 and ran 400. Each experiment also has a validity check:
the attacker must raise the regret of nominal EIG, otherwise the run is \emph{invalid}.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} For each experiment the protocol, gates, configuration, gate code and tests were committed
before any calibration or main run; the runners refuse a dirty tree, a changed configuration or an existing output, and
the analysis scripts refuse to overwrite a verdict. Table~\ref{tab:exp} lists the four experiments.

\subsection{Negative results}\label{sec:neg}

\textbf{E1: decision-valued probing.} $P$ beat $J$ on the pooled known attackers (RRR $\numLJ$ [$\numLJlo$, $\numLJhi$]),
but the experiment was killed by three gates. Against $C$, which uses the same joint model with plain $H$-information,
the gain vanished ($\numLC$ [$\numLClo$, $\numLChi$]). On the held-out misattribution attacker it was negative
($\numLJmis$ [$\numLJmislo$, $\numLJmishi$]). The pooled gain came from the design attacker, whose behaviour matches the
defender's compromised-source model ($\numLJcov$ [$\numLJcovlo$, $\numLJcovhi$]). Probing was also untargeted: only about a
third of $P$'s probes went to sources the posterior considered suspicious, against a pre-registered floor of one half.

\textbf{E2: robust acquisition.} E1's exploratory data suggested that model-based policies fail when the attacker does
not match their model. E2 tested two principled fixes, each changing $J$ in one way: sampling actions in proportion to
their score, and scoring by the worst case over three compromised-source models. On a new held-out attacker neither
beat $J$: randomisation lost clearly ($\numTR$ [$\numTRlo$, $\numTRhi$]) and the minimax policy tied ($\numTM$
[$\numTMlo$, $\numTMhi$]) while losing on the known families.

\subsection{Scoped positive result}\label{sec:pos}

\textbf{Origin, disclosed.} In E1 and E2, $C$ had markedly lower regret than both $R$ and $J$ in exploratory tables.
E3 tested this prospectively, on new world seeds and a new held-out attacker (collude-cheap) written before any E3 run.
No policy, threshold or world parameter was changed.

\textbf{E3: joint belief.} Every gate passed. On collude-cheap, $C$ reduced regret by $\numUR$ [$\numURlo$, $\numURhi$]
relative to $R$ and by $\numUJ$ [$\numUJlo$, $\numUJhi$] relative to $J$, with a positive RRR in each attacker budget and
in both halves of the worlds, and lower regret than $R$ on clean worlds. Because $C$ and $R$ share the objective,
horizon, cost handling, posterior, decision and spend, the gain is attributable to the look-ahead belief model:
modelling that a compromised source reports a cover-up, and that its state persists and is correlated with $H$, rather
than treating it as symmetric noise independent of $H$.

\textbf{Where it stops.} Fig.~\ref{fig:scope} and Table~\ref{tab:regret} show the limits, which travel with the claim.
(i) Against misattribution the gain over $R$ is small ($\numURmis$ [$\numURmislo$, $\numURmishi$]). (ii) Against the
adaptive trust-harvest attacker there is no gain over $J$ ($\numUJth$ [$\numUJthlo$, $\numUJthhi$]), and random
acquisition has the lowest regret of all policies; the same held in E1 and E2. (iii) $C$ is not the best policy on
collude-cheap: nominal EIG, which ignores cost, avoids the cheap sources the attacker controls and does better. The
pre-registered claim is a comparison among cost-aware policies, not ``best against this attacker''.

\section{Real-Data Attempt: HAI}\label{sec:hai}

\textbf{Mapping.} HAI is a public hardware-in-the-loop industrial-control dataset with labelled attacks
\cite{shin2020hai,shin2021hai}; several of its scenarios hold a process variable at a stale value while the process
moves, which is a real cover-up. We used HAI 20.07 for development (detectors and model fitting, \numVndev\ episodes),
HAI 21.03 for the verdict and HAI 22.04 as secondary. An episode is one attack segment or a sampled normal window;
hypotheses are normal, attack confined to the boiler process, or attack touching the other processes. Nine process
variables and controller outputs are sources. A query reads a source's change alarm over a 30-second window (threshold
at the 99th percentile of attack-free training data); a probe checks the source against a ridge regression on the
other points of its process. The $k$-th read of a source returns the $k$-th window, up to six reads. The defender model
was fitted on development episodes only and applied unchanged; the protocol was committed before any confirmatory
episode was built, and an in-sample look at the development data was disclosed in it.

\textbf{Outcome: invalid.} The validity check requires nominal EIG to beat a decision taken from the prior alone. It did
not: on 21.03 the difference was $\numVoneA$ [$\numVoneloA$, $\numVonehiA$] (\numVnA\ episodes, \numVattA\ attacks,
\numVspA\ with spoofing), and on 22.04 every evidence-reading policy did worse than the prior ($\numVoneB$
[$\numVoneloB$, $\numVonehiB$]). The comparison of belief models is therefore not tested. Table~\ref{tab:hai} shows
why: missed boiler attacks dominate regret, no deterministic policy ever probed (the development probe failed
$\numVfailh$ of healthy and $\numVfails$ of spoofed readings, at twice a query's cost), and the evidence is weak.

\textbf{An exploratory warning.} Although not interpretable under the protocol, on 21.03 $J$ had lower regret than $C$
($\numVJA$ [$\numVJloA$, $\numVJhiA$]); on 22.04 the two were level ($\numVJB$ [$\numVJloB$, $\numVJhiB$]). If a future
replication passes the validity check, the E3 result could fail there. We do not re-run E4; a new attempt needs a new
protocol and a detector that passes the validity check first.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{External validity.} E1--E3 use one simulator family and one world distribution. The only real-data attempt
was invalid, and its exploratory direction on 21.03 disagreed with E3. The positive result must be read as
simulator-only.

\textbf{Model match.} The defender's compromised-source model is the cover-up model. Attackers that lie in that
direction (cover-up, decoy, collude) are where $C$ gains most; misattribution and adaptive attackers are where it does
not. Held-out attackers reduce, but do not remove, this bias: the same researcher wrote the policies and the attackers.

\textbf{Exploratory origin.} E3's hypothesis came from E1 and E2 data after their verdicts. E3 is its first
prospective test, with new seeds and a new attacker; a second independent test has not been done.

\textbf{Construct validity.} Regret is normalised per world and RRR is a ratio of sums, so worlds with large regret
weigh more. Results are reported per budget and per world half to show the effect is not driven by a few worlds. The
HAI mapping (change alarms, a regression probe, three coarse hypotheses) is one of many possible and may be too weak.

\textbf{Multiplicity.} Each experiment controls its own error rate (intersection--union across comparators; Bonferroni
across E2's two candidates). Across the four experiments we report all outcomes, including the failures.

\section{Conclusion}

When sources may lie, what helped in our experiments was not a cleverer objective or a robustness wrapper but a better
look-ahead model: one that represents each source's compromise state jointly with the hypothesis. That result is scoped
to non-adaptive attackers whose lies resemble the defender's model, it vanishes against an attacker that exploits the
defender's choices (where random acquisition is best), and it is not yet supported by real data. Decision-valued
probing and robust acquisition both failed pre-registered tests. We hope the spend-matched, held-out-attacker protocol
is useful beyond these results: in this problem, three of four ideas that looked good on the data that suggested them
did not survive a test fixed in advance.
